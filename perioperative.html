Designing Perioperative

An end-to-end concept for a patient-facing app that would help a plastic surgery clinical chain eliminate miscommunication, reduce liability, and give patients full control of their care journey.

My role Lead Designer
Team Lead designer, supporting designer, project supervisor
Platform iOS (with web portal scoped)
Timeline 2 weeks (proof of concept)
Deliverables Brand, User Flows, Prototype, Deck
Status Concept (client lost funding)

The problem

Perioperative care covers the full arc of a surgical patient's experience: preparation before the procedure, the procedure itself, and recovery after it. In a plastic surgery context, this arc can span weeks or months, and every stage carries a specific set of instructions, medications, lifestyle adjustments, and clinical checkpoints that the patient is expected to follow.

The clinical chain that commissioned this work had identified a recurring and serious problem. Miscommunication between provider and patient across this arc was generating real risk: patients misunderstanding pre-op instructions, missing medication schedules, failing to follow post-op protocols, and arriving underprepared for procedures. The downstream consequences ranged from poor clinical outcomes to liability exposure for the provider.

Their existing approach relied on printed handouts, verbal instructions, and follow-up calls. It was fragmented, undocumented, and entirely dependent on the patient's ability to retain and act on information delivered in a clinical setting, often at a moment of high anxiety.

"The problem was not that patients did not care. It was that the information they needed was scattered, difficult to act on, and delivered at exactly the wrong moment."

What they wanted

The client came to Optera with a request for a proof of concept: a patient-facing mobile application that would consolidate the full perioperative care experience into a single tool. If the concept landed, it would lead to a full product build including a clinical management dashboard and administrative portal. We were given complete creative latitude. Research and personas had already been prepared on their end.

My role

I was the lead designer on this engagement. All design decisions, user flows, information architecture, branding, and visual design were mine. A second designer on the team experimented with alternative ideas informally, which helped sharpen the structure, but the final direction was mine throughout. In my project supervisor's words: the project was mine and he barely touched it.

The patient journey

One of the most important pieces of strategic work on this project was mapping the full patient journey from first clinical contact through to post-operative recovery. The journey runs thirteen steps across three phases, and it was this map that determined what the application needed to do and when it needed to do it.

What became clear through this process was that the app's role was not just informational. It needed to be an active participant in the care journey: creating reminders, surfacing instructions at the right moment, integrating with the patient's personal schedule, and generating a portable medical record as a byproduct of use. In other words, it had to function as a service layer, not just a content delivery tool.

The journey made something visible that was not in the original brief: the application was not just a companion tool. It was doing the clinical communication work that the provider had previously done through verbal instruction and paper handouts. By step 13, it was also generating a portable medical record. The design had to carry that weight.

Information architecture and user flows

With the journey in hand, the next step was defining what the application needed to contain and how a user would move through it. I developed two levels of flow: a site map capturing the full information architecture, and a detailed user flow charting every decision point and state transition across the main user paths.

The architecture resolved into four primary sections accessible from the main navigation: Home, Calendar, My Health, and Surgeries. Each section served a distinct function in the care journey, but they connected to each other in meaningful ways: a surgery selected in the Surgeries section would feed into the Calendar, which would generate tasks and reminders that surfaced on the Home dashboard. The app was designed to feel like one coherent system rather than four separate tools bundled together.

Branding

The brief required a full brand alongside the product design. The name Perioperative was given; everything else needed to be built from scratch.

The visual identity needed to communicate clinical credibility without feeling cold or institutional. Healthcare design has a long history of defaulting to sterile blues and hard edges. The goal here was something warmer: trustworthy enough for a medical context, approachable enough for a patient who is anxious about a procedure they have never been through before.

The logo mark uses two overlapping leaf-like forms in blue and green, suggesting continuity and growth, sitting comfortably next to a clean, modern wordmark in the same dark blue. The color palette carried those two tones through the full interface.

The application

The application was designed entirely in high fidelity, from splash screen through to the full feature set. The visual language was clean, warm, and deliberately approachable, using illustrated characters throughout to soften the clinical content and reduce the anxiety a first-time surgical patient might bring to the experience.

Home dashboard

The home screen was designed around the patient's immediate context: their current health status, what is coming up in their surgery schedule, and what they need to do today. A daily check-in prompt surfaces every morning. The surgery timeline and daily tasks sit below it. Daily health stats anchor the bottom, giving the patient a sense of progress and control.

Calendar

The calendar integrated the patient's full care timeline: appointments, reminders, medication schedules, and to-do tasks in one view. Rather than requiring the patient to manually enter everything, the app populated the calendar from the surgery information and provider-supplied schedules, with the patient confirming and adding personal items on top.

Above: Calendar screen with integrated reminders and appointment management.

My Health

The health tracking section gave patients visibility into the lifestyle factors most relevant to surgical recovery: sleep, water intake, steps, calories, medicine intake, and daily mood. Progress bars made it easy to see at a glance where the patient stood against their daily targets. Each metric had its own detail view with logging tools and historical trends.

Above: Health tracking screen with daily progress overview and management tools.

Surgeries

The surgeries section served two functions. For patients who had not yet committed to a procedure, it was a discovery and education tool: a searchable, filterable library of procedures with detailed information about each one including pre-op care, post-op care, possible complications, and available surgeons. For patients who had already scheduled a surgery, it became a management view: connecting the procedure to their calendar, generating tasks, and tracking their progress through the care timeline.

The "I'm having this surgery" action in the flow was the bridge between these two modes, and a key design decision: it was the moment the application went from informational to active, moving the surgery into the patient's personal care plan and triggering the full care management functionality.

What was scoped but not built

The proof of concept was designed with the next phase in mind. The client's intention was to follow a successful RFP with a full product build that included a clinical management dashboard and administrative portal on the provider side. That would have turned Perioperative from a patient-facing app into a two-sided platform: one interface for patients managing their care, and one for the clinical team managing their patients.

The project did not get there. The client lost funding shortly after the concept was presented and the engagement was closed. The designs and prototype represent the full scope of what was completed.

This case study represents a complete, high-fidelity proof of concept delivered under a two-week timeline, across brand, UX, and product design, for a clinical use case that was technically and regulatorily non-trivial. It did not ship. What it demonstrates is the ability to move quickly, hold strategic intent and design quality at the same time, and produce something credible enough that the next step would have been a full build.

Reflection

The most interesting design challenge on Perioperative was not any individual screen. It was recognizing early that the journey map was not just a research artifact. It was the actual product specification. Once the thirteen steps were clear, every feature decision followed logically. The application was, in essence, a digital translation of the care journey, with an interface layer on top.

The part of the work I would most like to have continued was the provider side. A patient app that operates without a connected clinical portal is only solving half the problem. The real opportunity was in closing the loop between patient and provider, making the patient's behavior and progress visible to the care team in real time. That was the system the client originally had in mind, and it remains an interesting design problem.
