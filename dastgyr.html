Building a logistics ecosystem from the inside out

Product Design Service Design

How a fragmented, third-party-dependent delivery operation was rebuilt into a unified, in-house logistics system spanning drivers, warehouses, and management.

Product Designer, Design Lead (Driver App)

Logistics, E-Commerce

The situation

Dastgyr is a VC-backed B2B e-commerce platform connecting retailers to suppliers across Pakistan. By late 2022, the company had been operational for close to two years, and having been engineering-first, had relied on its engineers to build internal tools and processes. When a design team was assembled, it quickly became clear that several legacy systems had accumulated significant technical debt.

The delivery operation was one of the most critical. Dastgyr had scaled through a lot of bootstrapping, creating manual processes for everything from driver attendance to multi-site pickups and deliveries. Some of those processes had been partially digitized through a mobile app and a set of operations dashboards, but the systems were siloed. Delivery allocation, labor pay reconciliation, and cash reconciliation all took far longer than they should have. The driver app itself had been built without design input, was visually outdated, and had been nearly abandoned.

A company-wide design audit surfaced the full extent of the problem and made the case for allocating resources to a proper overhaul rather than continuing to layer new features on top of a broken foundation.

The initiative

The response was a two-part initiative. First, a proprietary routing engine built by two in-house data scientists on open-source infrastructure, replacing an expensive and inflexible third-party system. A single run of that third-party tool could take hours, any change required restarting the process from scratch, and the API costs were unsustainable at Dastgyr's volume.

Second, the design work required to make the engine usable across every touchpoint in the logistics operation. I joined the project mid-way through, brought on specifically to design this front-end layer. My scope covered the driver app end to end, key features in the admin and logistics portals, and the service design connecting them.

The driver app

The existing driver app was a shelved, partially-deployed product: visually outdated, functionally incomplete, and built on a bootstrapped codebase with no design foundation. The challenge was not to start from scratch. The engineering side was already partially deployed, which meant user flows could not be redesigned wholesale. Drivers had familiarity with the old version, and rebuilding the flows entirely would have required retraining a workforce with limited bandwidth for it.

The goal was to simplify the pickup and delivery process, optimize it for the drivers, and bring the manual processes into a digital ecosystem that would connect the company's internal functions.

Research

We started by defining which functions needed to be consolidated and building a user flow that would integrate them into the new application. Research involved conversations with warehouse operations teams, driver teams, and operations stakeholders to understand how the delivery process actually worked and when the app was being used in the field.

One of the more significant findings came from pulling analytics data. The phones our drivers were using had screens smaller than the market average. This was not an assumption we would have made without looking at the data, and it changed a number of fundamental design decisions that followed.

Above: a partial user flow diagram and the mobile application development process.

Constraints

Engineering lock-in

The codebase was already partially deployed. The design had to work within the existing structural logic rather than rebuilding what engineering had already shipped.

Non-standard screen size

Analytics data revealed drivers were using phones with screens smaller than the market average, a constraint invisible until we looked at the data.

In-motion use

The app would be checked while driving or at quick stops. Critical information had to be legible at a glance, with no important actions buried below a scroll.

Compressed timeline

Design and development had to be completed in roughly two months to align with the routing engine deployment schedule.

Design principles

Rather than applying generic heuristics, we derived a small set of rules directly from the constraints and context of the work.

  • 01Anything critical belongs above the fold. No important buttons behind a scroll.
  • 02Screens surface contextually based on driver status, not manual navigation.
  • 03The app functions primarily as a sequencing tool, not an information hub.
  • 04Floating action buttons for high-frequency, time-sensitive tasks.
  • 05Each screen shows what is next, not what just happened.

The design system decision

The existing visual language was too degraded to update incrementally. It was cramped, inconsistent, and unreadable in field conditions. We made the decision to build a new design system for the driver app: one that was legible, systematically structured, and designed for the screen sizes our drivers were actually using.

This foundation became the starting point for a broader design system initiative I launched for all Dastgyr products. I transitioned out for graduate school before it was complete. The team finished it after my departure.

What the app needed to do

The redesign consolidated a number of previously fragmented or manual functions into a single user flow.

  • 01Self-marked attendance based on proximity to pickup locations
  • 02Simplified menus and navigation for use while driving
  • 03Proximity-based delivery confirmation to ensure correct deliveries
  • 04Redundancies built into the flow for missed deliveries and skipped stops
  • 05Two-way confirmation systems for cash entry and reconciliation
  • 06An optimized spot sales feature
  • 07On-route cash deposit stops, removing the need for end-of-day bank runs
  • 08A performance tracking mechanism giving drivers visibility into their own earnings and payouts

Testing in the field

We tested with five drivers of varied backgrounds in parked trucks, as close to real conditions as scheduling would allow. Drivers told us directly what read clearly, what got missed, and what sequence felt natural. Feedback gathered after deployment was handed off to the team when I transitioned out.

The routing engine front-end

The routing engine was built by two data scientists, Hamza Azeem and Hassaan Hasan. My role was to design the interfaces through which that engine would be operated, monitored, and overridden by the people running Dastgyr's logistics operation: primarily warehouse managers and delivery coordinators.

Before screens could be designed, the system's requirements had to be defined at each touchpoint. That meant mapping what each user type needed to see, when they needed to see it, and what interventions needed to be available in real time, such as handling a closed shop, a traffic jam, or a cash deposit stop.

The cash deposit problem

Research interviews with the accounting and finance team and warehouse managers surfaced a recurring operational pain. Drivers were returning to the warehouse each day carrying cash collected from retailers. Someone had to make a separate bank run. Reconciliation was slow and error-prone. Discrepancies were common.

The solution was to build bank and cash deposit machine stops directly into the driver's route as first-class waypoints in the algorithm rather than manual additions. This change reduced cash reconciliation time by 98 percent, eliminated manual bank runs, and significantly reduced cash discrepancy rates.

Admin and logistics portals

The admin and warehouse logistics portals needed to support:

  • 01Route configuration and creation by logistics managers, with algorithm-generated suggestions
  • 02Real-time route tracking with deviation and idle time detection
  • 03Manual override and editing without restarting the full process
  • 04Loading mechanisms to inform warehouse staff which orders go on which vehicles
  • 05Customer-facing delivery notifications
  • 06Cash deposit stop integration within driver routes

Above: admin and logistics portal screens for route configuration and real-time tracking.

Design approach

To minimize engineering overhead, most admin portal designs were kept at low fidelity, leveraging existing system components wherever possible. The driver app required higher-fidelity treatment given the conditions it was designed for. The two products were built in parallel and deployed together.

Outcomes

The routing engine and driver app went live together. The impact was immediate across multiple dimensions that had previously been accepted as fixed costs of running the operation at scale.

Decrease in reconciliation time

Total time spent on cash reconciliation after deliveries dropped 98 percent, from 12 hours (a full working day) down to 15 minutes, since most cash was already accounted for during the routes and the data was already available in the system.

Elimination of third-party costs

The routing engine meant Dastgyr no longer relied on third-party software and external APIs, removing those costs from operational overhead entirely.

Decrease in route processing time

New route generation and processing time went down as services were brought in-house and integrated into the system. Managers could deploy new routes in real time rather than manually building and validating sheets.

Efficiency KPIs met

Dastgyr's delivery efficiency KPIs, including benchmarks for fuel consumption, delivery times, kilometers traveled, and delivery success rates, all improved against third-party scores.

Internal systems began speaking to each other for the first time. Logistics, warehouse management, driver operations, and finance were brought into a single connected workflow. Reconciliation, payout, and delivery processes saw increased automation, reduced manual labor, and fewer errors across the board.

The design system work started during this project was later expanded into a company-wide standardization effort, completed by the team after my departure.
